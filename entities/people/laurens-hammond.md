# Laurens Hammond (1895–1973)

American mechanical engineer and prolific inventor, born 1895 in Evanston, Illinois. Cornell engineering graduate (1916), WWI veteran, and founder of the [Hammond Clock Company](/entities/manufacturers/hammond-organ-company.md) (1928).

## Path to the organ
- Invented a **synchronous motor** that ran silently and steadily on the new 60-cycle household AC supply — the key enabler of both his silent electric clock and the later organ.
- Other inventions: one of the first successful **3D movie systems**, an automated electric bridge table (a Depression-era product whose decline pushed him to look for a new hit).
- Inspiration for the organ: the musical tones produced by the moving gears of his electric clocks; he combined a $15 second-hand piano with a compact tonewheel generator descended from Cahill's Telharmonium.
- Not a musician himself — he had the company's assistant treasurer (trained organist W. L. Lahey) help voice the instrument.[1]

## The organ
Filed the patent on **April 24, 1934** (co-inventor John M. Hanert), unveiled April 1935, first [Model A](/entities/instruments/hammond-b3.md) shipped June 1935. Henry Ford placed the first order (six organs) before production even started — but serial #1 went to a Kansas City dealer and ended up in the Smithsonian.[2]

To cut costs he used a 25-note pedalboard instead of the church-standard 32 — it became the de facto standard for decades.[1]

## Legacy
He declined to market Don [Leslie](/entities/people/donald-leslie.md)'s rotating speaker and even made the console connectors "Leslie-proof" — a rare misjudgment, as the [Leslie speaker](/entities/instruments/leslie-speaker.md) became inseparable from the organ's identity.
