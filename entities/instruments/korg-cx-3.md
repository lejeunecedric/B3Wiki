# Korg CX-3 (1980)

Single-manual analog clonewheel organ from [Korg](/entities/manufacturers/korg.md) — the company first dedicated Hammond-style instrument and the one credited, by journalist Gordon Reid, with having "come close to emulating the true depth and passion of a vintage Hammond."[1]

## Position in the family
- **Korg CX-3** (1980) — the analog original.
- **Korg BX-3** (1980) — dual-manual sibling, also analog.
- **Korg New CX-3** / **New BX-3** (2000) — re-released with updated modeling and effects for the 2000s.[1]

## Why it matters
The CX-3 and the [Roland VK-1](/entities/manufacturers/roland-corporation.md) (released the same year) mark the moment where early analog "Hammond-shaped" clonewheels started to credibly imitate a real B-3 — after decades of synthesizer-voiced attempts that failed to capture the upper harmonics and Leslie behavior. Both were widely adopted by touring bands and club-date organists who could not lug a real B-3 around.[1]

## New CX-3 (2001): the physical-modeling re-release

Korg re-reissued the CX-3 name in January 2001 as a *digital* instrument — a "tone wheel organ modelling tone generator" with REMS-modeled effects — in a vintage-styled wooden case. Gordon Reid's *Sound on Sound* review called it "the best emulation of the Hammond C3 I've yet heard," singling out its key-click "spit" and emulation of the vintage organ's subtle touch-sensitivity (some notes "speak" fully on fast playing, others just clunk and click). Its **EX mode** extended the classic nine drawbars with four programmable harmonic drawbars (up to the 24th harmonic) and five fixed percussion settings — "65 years after the introduction of the original, Korg have actually improved upon the 'algorithm' of the tonewheel organ." Its omissions — no Leslie output, no spring reverb algorithm, bitimbral-only (two manuals + pedals impossible) — set the agenda for the next clonewheel generation (see [B-3 vs Clonewheels](/comparisons/b3-vs-clonewheels.md)).[2]
