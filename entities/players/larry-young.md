# Larry Young (Khalid Yasin)

American jazz organist (October 7, 1940, Newark, New Jersey – March 30, 1978, New York City), also known as Khalid Yasin (Abdul Aziz). Where the rest of the 1960s soul-jazz legion stayed in [Jimmy Smith](/entities/players/jimmy-smith.md)'s groove-centered shadow, Young took the [B-3](/entities/instruments/hammond-b3.md) the other way — toward [Blue Note](/entities/labels/blue-note-records.md)'s most exploratory records and the first wave of jazz fusion.[1][2]

## Career

Young came up through Newark Arts High School and 1950s R&B bands, and studied piano before the organ. He recorded with Jimmy Forrest in 1960 and led a string of Prestige/New Jazz soul-jazz dates — *Testifying*, *Young Blues*, *Groove Street* (1960–1962) — on which he still sounded close to Smith.[1][2]

Signing with Blue Note around 1964 marked the turn: the influence of John Coltrane's modal music came to dominate his playing and composing. *Into Somethin'* (rec. November 1964, with Sam Rivers, Grant Green and Elvin Jones) was his Blue Note debut, and *Unity* (rec. 1965, with Joe Henderson and the young Woody Shaw) remains his best-known album — AllMusic's Ron Wynn calls his approach "as radical... in the '60s as Jimmy Smith had posed in the '50s."[1][2] His later Blue Note dates (*Of Love and Peace*, *Contrasts*, *Heaven on Earth*, *Mother Ship*) drew on the 1960s avant-garde and on musicians from his hometown Newark.[1]

He then joined the first wave of fusion: on *Emergency!* with the Tony Williams Lifetime (with Tony Williams and John McLaughlin) — where his percussive organ and synth-like effects defined the band's sound — and on Miles Davis's *Bitches Brew* (1969). A jam with Jimi Hendrix surfaced posthumously on *Nine to the Universe*.[1][2]

In March 1978 he entered a New York hospital for stomach pains and died there on March 30, 1978, aged 37; the reported cause (pneumonia) is disputed, and both sources treat the actual cause as unclear.[1]

## Place in the organ tradition

Young is the counter-example inside the soul-jazz golden age documented in [who carried the B-3 beyond Jimmy Smith](/queries/b3-beyond-jimmy-smith.md): same instrument, same label family as Smith ([Blue Note Records](/entities/labels/blue-note-records.md)), but waves of modal harmony and fusion energy instead of blues-and-fatback. He belongs on the Coltrane branch of the organ's family tree, not the gospel branch described in [the gospel lineage](/queries/hammond-gospel-lineage.md).
