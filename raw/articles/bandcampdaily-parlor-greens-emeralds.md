[ALBUM OF THE DAY](/album-of-the-day) Parlor Greens, “Emeralds” By [Blake Gillespie](/contributors/blake-gillespie) · March 26, 2026 ​

​

Reverence for the organ trio tradition of 1970s soul jazz remains [Parlor Greens](https://parlorgreens.bandcamp.com/)’ guiding light on sophomore album [*Emeralds*](https://parlorgreens.bandcamp.com/album/emeralds). The trio was founded when Tim Carman—whose heavy blues rock trio, [GA-20](https://ga-20.bandcamp.com/music), releases music through [Colemine](https://coleminerecords.bandcamp.com/)’s catch-all subsidiary Karma Chief—told [Colemine founder Terry Cole](https://daily.bandcamp.com/label-profile/colemine-records-label-guide) about his aspirations for an organ trio after which Cole called up guitarist Jimmy James and organ player [Adam Scone.](https://adamscone.bandcamp.com/album/low-slow) James did a seven-year stint in the [Delvon Lamarr Organ Trio,](https://delvonlamarrorgantrio.bandcamp.com/) while Scone played an integral role in the late ’90s soul revival as a member of [The Sugarman 3](https://thesugarman3.bandcamp.com/) and as a session player with [Daptone](https://daptonerecords.bandcamp.com/). Not only had James and Scone played in organ trios in the past, but they’d previously collaborated on [*Brooklyn to Brooklin*](https://sconecashplayers.bandcamp.com/album/brooklyn-to-brooklin), the 2022 full-length from Scone Cash Players.

If 2024 debut [*In Green We Dream*](https://parlorgreens.bandcamp.com/album/in-green-we-dream) was Parlor Greens getting acquainted, particularly in determining Carman’s stylistic role, *Emeralds* is the trio moving in unison from the theoretical to a united, experiential understanding of their dynamic. No longer the interloper, Carman is channeling directly from the soul jazz source on “Letter To Brother Ben,” which adopts the stylings of jazz drummer Ben Dixon. For lead single “Eat Your Greens,” he’s got the master of jazz-infused funk drumming [Idris Muhammad](https://idrismuhammad.bandcamp.com/album/peace-and-rhythm) on his mind. Both tracks place the trio firmly in that pocket of 1970s Blue Note and Prestige groups like [Charles Earland](https://charlesearland.bandcamp.com/album/black-drops), [Richard “Groove” Holmes](https://richardgrooveholmes.bandcamp.com/album/on-basies-bandstand), and [Brother Jack McDuff.](https://brotherjackmcduff.bandcamp.com/album/ain-t-no-sunshine-live-in-seattle) Occasionally, though, their work sways more toward jazz and blues than the funk of their debut. Scone rides into “Lion’s Mane” on Horace Silver’s “Song For My Father” refrain, but it’s a momentary fit, a foundation for the trio to build upon. By the time James lets the solo rip, Scone and Carman fall back into a trance state reminiscent of *Band of Gypsies*-era Hendrix, a mood reprised on the roadhouse-ready “Red Dog.” These touchstones invite the blues background of James and Carman into the conversation in ways that enrich Parlor Greens’ identity as more than revivalists.

Parlor Greens’ range as instrumentalists, reinforced by their attention to emotional and dynamic contrast, ensures *Emeralds*’ nostalgic vibes stay fresh, never stale or safe. Whether they’re serving up Easter eggs (James’s guitar on “Drop Top” moves with the same sobering cadence of William Bell’s “I Forgot To Be Your Lover”), covering Dolly Parton (“Jolene,” obviously), or just putting their personal stamp on tradition, the band have given us nothing but pristine gems on *Emeralds*. Organ music—and instrumental soul jazz in general—doesn’t get better than this.
