Musical artist

**James Harrell McGriff** (April 3, 1936 – May 24, 2008)[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-ALLMUSIC-1) was an American [hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop "Hard bop") and [soul-jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul-jazz "Soul-jazz") [organist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organist "Organist") and organ trio bandleader.

## Biography

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jimmy_McGriff&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Biography")\]

### Early years and influences

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jimmy_McGriff&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Early years and influences")\]

Born in [Germantown, Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germantown,_Philadelphia "Germantown, Philadelphia"), Pennsylvania,[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-Larkin-2) McGriff started playing piano at the age of five and by his teens had also learned to play [vibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibraphone "Vibraphone"), alto sax, drums and [upright bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upright_bass "Upright bass").[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-go-3) He played bass in his first group, a piano trio. When he joined the United States Army, McGriff served as a [military policeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_police "Military police") during the [Korean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War "Korean War"). He later became a police officer in Philadelphia for two years.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-ALLMUSIC-1)

Music kept drawing McGriff's attention away from the police force. His childhood friend, organist [Jimmy Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Smith_(musician) "Jimmy Smith (musician)"), had begun earning a substantial reputation in jazz for his [Blue Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Note_Records "Blue Note Records") albums (the two played together once in 1967) and McGriff became entranced by the organ sound while [Richard "Groove" Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_%22Groove%22_Holmes "Richard \"Groove\" Holmes") played at his sister's wedding. Holmes went on to become McGriff's teacher and friend and they recorded together on two occasions in 1973 for two [Groove Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groove_Merchant "Groove Merchant") records.

McGriff bought his first [Hammond B-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammond_B-3 "Hammond B-3") organ in 1956, spent six months learning the instrument, then studied at New York's [Juilliard School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juilliard_School "Juilliard School"). He also studied privately with [Milt Buckner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milt_Buckner "Milt Buckner"), [Jimmy Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Smith_(musician) "Jimmy Smith (musician)"), and [Sonny Gatewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Gatewood?action=edit&redlink=1 "Sonny Gatewood (page does not exist)"). He was influenced by the energy and dynamics of organist Buckner and the diplomatic aplomb of [Count Basie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Basie "Count Basie"), and by local organists such as Howard "The Demon" Whaley and Austin Mitchell.

### 1960s: First combos

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jimmy_McGriff&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: 1960s: First combos")\]

McGriff formed a combo that played around Philadelphia and often featured tenor saxophonist [Charles Earland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Earland "Charles Earland") (who soon switched permanently to organ, and became one of the instrument's renowned performers). During this time, McGriff also accompanied such artists as [Don Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Gardner "Don Gardner"), [Arthur Prysock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Prysock "Arthur Prysock"), [Candido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candido "Candido") and [Carmen McRae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmen_McRae "Carmen McRae"), who came through town for local club dates.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-ALLMUSIC-1)

In 1961, McGriff's trio was offered the chance to record an instrumental version of [Ray Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Charles "Ray Charles")' hit " [I've Got a Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I've_Got_a_Woman "I've Got a Woman")" by Joe Lederman's Jell Records, a small independent label.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-Larkin-2) When the record received substantial local airplay, [Juggy Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juggy_Murray "Juggy Murray")'s [Sue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sue_Records "Sue Records") label picked it up and recorded a full album of McGriff's trio, released in 1962.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-Larkin-2) The album also turned out another hit in McGriff's "All About My Girl",[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-go-3) establishing McGriff's credentials as a fiery blues-based organist, well-versed in [gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_music "Gospel music"), [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_music "Soul music") and "fatback groove".

McGriff recorded a series of popular albums for the Sue label between 1962 and 1965, ending with what still stands as one of his finest examples of blues-based jazz, _[Blues for Mister Jimmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues_for_Mister_Jimmy "Blues for Mister Jimmy")_. When producer [Sonny Lester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Lester "Sonny Lester") started his [Solid State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_State_Records_(jazz_label) "Solid State Records (jazz label)") record label in 1966, he recruited McGriff to be his star attraction. Lester framed McGriff in many different groups, performing a wide variety of styles and giving the organist nearly unlimited opportunities to record. McGriff was heard everywhere from an all-star tribute to Count Basie: _The Big Band_ (1966), a series of "organ and blues band" albums such as _Honey_ (1968) and _A Thing to Come By_ (1969), funk classics like _Electric Funk_ (1970), covering pop hits ("Cherry", "Blue Moon", "The Way You Look Tonight") and such original singles as "The Worm" and "Step One".

During this time, McGriff performed at clubs and concert halls worldwide. He settled in [Newark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey "Newark, New Jersey"), [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"), and eventually opened his own supper club, The Golden Slipper - where he recorded _Black Pearl_ and another live album, _Chicken Fried Soul_ with [Junior Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_Parker "Junior Parker") in 1971. Beginning in 1969, he also performed regularly with [Buddy Rich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddy_Rich "Buddy Rich")'s band, though the two were only recorded once together in 1974 on _[The Last Blues Album Volume 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Blues_Album_Volume_1 "The Last Blues Album Volume 1")_.

### 1970s–1980s

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jimmy_McGriff&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: 1970s–1980s")\]

McGriff "retired" from the music industry in 1972 to start a horse farm in Connecticut. But Sonny Lester's new record company, [Groove Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groove_Merchant "Groove Merchant"), kept issuing McGriff records at a rate of three or four a year. By 1973, McGriff was touring relentlessly and actively recording again. Around this time, [disco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco "Disco") was gaining a hold in jazz music and McGriff's flexibility proved infallible.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-go-3) He produced some of his best music during this period: _Stump Juice_ (1975), _The Mean Machine_ (1976), _Red Beans_ (1976) and _Outside Looking In_ (1978). These albums still stand out today as excellent documents of McGriff's organ playing.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-ALLMUSIC-1)

By 1980, McGriff broke away from Sonny Lester and began working actively with producer [Bob Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Porter_(record_producer) "Bob Porter (record producer)") (and recording engineer [Rudy Van Gelder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Van_Gelder "Rudy Van Gelder")). McGriff began a long relationship with [Milestone Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milestone_Records "Milestone Records") collaborating with [Rusty Bryant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rusty_Bryant "Rusty Bryant"), [Al Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Grey "Al Grey"), [Red Holloway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Holloway "Red Holloway"), [David "Fathead" Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_%22Fathead%22_Newman "David \"Fathead\" Newman"), [Frank Wess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Wess "Frank Wess") and [Eric Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Alexander_(jazz_saxophonist) "Eric Alexander (jazz saxophonist)").

In 1986, McGriff started a popular partnership with alto saxophone player [Hank Crawford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hank_Crawford "Hank Crawford"). Their partnership yielded five co-leader dates for Milestone records: _Soul Survivors_ (1986), _Steppin' Up_ (1987), _On the Blue Side_ (1989), _Road Tested_ (1997), and _Crunch Time_ (1999), as well as two dates for [Telarc Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telarc_International_Corporation "Telarc International Corporation"): _Right Turn on Blue_ (1994) and _Blues Groove_ (1995).

### 1990s–2000s

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jimmy_McGriff&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: 1990s–2000s")\]

Between 1994 and 1998, McGriff also experimented with the [Hammond XB-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammond_XB-3?action=edit&redlink=1 "Hammond XB-3 (page does not exist)"), an organ [synthesizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthesizer "Synthesizer") that increased the organ's capabilities with [MIDI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIDI "MIDI") enhancements.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-go-3) This gave McGriff an unnatural synthesized sound, which probably explains his retreat from the instrument on late recordings such as 2000's _McGriff's House Party_ (featuring fellow organist [Lonnie Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Lonnie_Smith_(musician) "Dr. Lonnie Smith (musician)")). _House Party_ did include the use of the XB-3; however, he did not use the [MIDI functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_Instrument_Digital_Interface "Musical Instrument Digital Interface").

McGriff was one of the first B3 players to add MIDI to the upper keyboard of his personal B3 to add and extend "his sound" beyond just the drawbar sound of the B3. He incorporated synthesizers in his live performances as he liked vibes, piano, strings, brass and other sounds that could only be created by a synthesizer and which the classic B3 cannot provide. Jimmy purchased the XB-3 as he had more control over the MIDI functions, and the XB-3 weighs about half of the classic B3, which made it easier to move.

Along with the [soul-jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul-jazz "Soul-jazz") sound, McGriff experienced renewed popularity in the mid-1990s, forming 'The Dream Team' group, which featured [David "Fathead" Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_%22Fathead%22_Newman "David \"Fathead\" Newman") (a longtime saxophonist with [Ray Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Charles "Ray Charles")) and drummer [Bernard Purdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Purdie "Bernard Purdie"), and recording _The Dream Team_ (1997), _Straight Up_ (1998), _McGriff's House Party_ (2000), _Feelin' It_ (2001), and _McGriff Avenue_ (2002) albums.

On March 29, 2008, McGriff was given a last private concert by Bill "Mr. B3" Dilks and drummer Grant MacAvoy in his honor in [Voorhees Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voorhees_Township,_New_Jersey "Voorhees Township, New Jersey"), [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"). Dilks brought his B3 and played for McGriff, his wife Margaret, their guests, and the folks at the Genesis Health Care Center. As Dilks said, "The Hammond reaches its players far beyond where the conscious mind lives".

A resident of Voorhees Township, New Jersey, McGriff died there at age 72 on May 24, 2008, of complications of [multiple sclerosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_sclerosis "Multiple sclerosis").[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_McGriff#cite_note-6)
