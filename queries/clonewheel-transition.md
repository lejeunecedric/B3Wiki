# The clonewheel transition: how digital organs displaced the tonewheel

**Question:** why and how did digital "clonewheel" instruments replace the electromechanical Hammond as the working organist's instrument?

## Answer in one line

The transition happened in three waves — analog approximations (1979–80s), affordable DSP modeling (1990s–2001), and full-fidelity modeling + touch (2002–2016) — driven less by sound than by logistics: a B-3 weighs 140 kg and production stopped in 1974.[^sos-hammond-xk-5-2018]

## Wave 1: analog approximations (1979–1984)

Korg's CX-3/BX-3 (1979/1980) were the first clones to credibly imitate a tonewheel Hammond — Gordon Reid judged them "a cut above" the Roland VK-1/VK-9 and Yamaha combo voices of the day, and by 2001 a good used BX-3 cost more than a real Hammond L-100 or M-100. But they were analog compromises, not models of the machine.[^sos-korg-cx3-2001] By 1985 the [Hammond Organ Company](/entities/manufacturers/hammond-organ-company.md) itself had abandoned tonewheels for integrated circuits, failed commercially, and folded; the name went to Noel Crabbe's Hammond Organ Australia and then to [Suzuki](/entities/manufacturers/suzuki-musical-instrument.md).[^wikipedia-hammond-organ]

## Wave 2: DSP modeling reaches credibility (1986–2001)

Hammond-Suzuki's first digital Hammond was the "Super B" (1986), developed through the XB-series into the 1990s. The decisive moment was Korg's 2001 re-release of the CX-3 as a *physical-modeling* instrument: Reid, reviewing it for *Sound on Sound*, called it "the best emulation of the Hammond C3 I've yet heard," praising in particular its emulation of key-click "spit" and the subtle touch-sensitivity of the vintage key contacts — the exact details previous emulators got wrong.[^sos-korg-cx3-2001] Its limits (no Leslie output, no spring reverb, bitimbral-only) also mapped out what the next generation had to fix. Hammond-Suzuki answered with the [XK-3](/entities/instruments/hammond-xk-3.md) (2004) and the flagship "New B-3" (2003, VASE III engine).[^sos-hammond-xk-5-2018]

## Wave 3: touch, not sound (2002–2016)

By the mid-2000s the *sound* was effectively solved; reviewers comparing Hammond-Suzuki's VASE III organs directly against a vintage A-100/Leslie 122RV found the emulation "remarkably faithful." What separated clones from the real thing was keyboard feel — the multi-busbar contact behavior behind glissandos, palm slides and partially-pressed notes. The [XK-5](/entities/instruments/hammond-xk-5.md) (2016) closed that gap with an in-house keybed and a three-contact system emulating nine staggered busbar contacts, plus per-tonewheel customization (leakage, filtering) down to sampling specific serial-numbered vintage organs. The reviewer's verdict: it became "arguably the best clonewheel money can buy."[^sos-hammond-xk-5-2018]

## What drove it

1. **Logistics**: 140 kg consoles, no production since 1974, and instruments "too precious, delicate, and valuable" to gig.[^sos-hammond-xk-5-2018]
2. **The effects chain mattered as much as the organ**: every credible clone had to model the Leslie, the vibrato/chorus scanner, tube preamp distortion and spring reverb, not just the drawbars — which is why [Leslie](/entities/instruments/leslie-speaker.md) emulation is the benchmark in every review.[^sos-korg-cx3-2001]
3. **Manufacturer pressure from both sides**: Suzuki owned the Hammond and Leslie brands and moved them digital, while Korg, Roland and Clavia competed from outside.[^wikipedia-clonewheel-organ]

## What was NOT lost

Firmware evolution shows the transition remains alive: the XK-5 launched as a purist console-Hammond-only instrument in 2016, and only added Vox/Farfisa/pipe voices with firmware 3.0 in April 2018 — evidence that the multi-voice "Swiss-army" stage keyboard (the SK series) and the purist single-purpose clone are now distinct product philosophies rather than a quality gap.[^sos-hammond-xk-5-2018]
