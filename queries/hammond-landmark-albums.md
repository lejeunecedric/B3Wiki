## Deep Purple — Jon Lord: the Hammond as a guitar

Lord's trademark sound emerged in the Mark II years (1969–73): a Hammond B-3/C-3 through a [[leslie-speaker|Leslie]] **driven into Marshall amplifiers**, deliberately distorted to match the attack and volume of Ritchie Blackmore's guitar — a "growling, heavy, mechanical" sound that let the organ compete as a lead voice rather than a backdrop.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-jon-lord.md] He ignored the Moog wave that Keith Emerson was riding and instead made the tonewheel organ the engine of hard rock: the organ riff idea behind "Child in Time", the call-and-answer classical-style duels with Blackmore on *In Rock* ("Speed King", "Bloodsucker"), and his own favourite studio solo — the police-siren "Hard Lovin' Man".^[raw/articles/wikipedia-jon-lord.md] *Machine Head* (1972) remains the signature document: "Smoke on the Water" rides Lord's rhythmic underpinning while "Highway Star", "Lazy" and "Pictures of Home" carry full organ solos.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-machine-head-album.md]^[raw/articles/wikipedia-jon-lord.md] *Burn* (1974) with the Coverdale/Hughes Mark III lineup is his overall playing on that album that stands out as a late-era peak.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-jon-lord.md]

## Emerson, Lake & Palmer — Keith Emerson: the Hammond as a percussion section

Emerson came to the Hammond through the same door as Lord — jazz-blues organ records — citing Jack McDuff's "Rock Candy" (also a stated Lord influence) as the trigger; his first Hammond was an **L-100**, a lighter spinet model he treated with legendary violence: knife-blades held on keys, machine-gun and explosion effects, instruments pushed across the stage.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-keith-emerson.md] In ELP (formed 1970 with Greg Lake and Carl Palmer after the Nice) the Hammond's role was different from Lord's: rather than one distorted wall of organ, Emerson paired the abused L-100 with the Moog synthesizer, making the two instruments trade lead duties.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-emerson-lake-palmer.md]^[raw/articles/wikipedia-keith-emerson.md] On the 1970 debut, "Lucky Man" ends with the canonical demonstration: a buzzing L-100 solo, then the first famous Moog solo rock audiences had heard. *Tarkus* (1971) and *Brain Salad Surgery* (1973) — "Karn Evil 9" — pushed the Hammond/Moog pairing through ever longer suite forms.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-emerson-lake-palmer.md]

## Mother Earth — identity note and honest coverage

**Disambiguation (verify first, because there are two bands with this name):** The band with the albums named in the query — *Living with the Animals* (Mercury, 1968) — is the **American** blues-rock band formed 1967 in California, fronted by singer Tracy Nelson out of San Francisco; they played the Fillmore/Avalon circuit and recorded four Mercury albums, with Mike Bloomfield guesting on *Living with the Animals* and Boz Scaggs on *Make A Joyful Noise* (1969).^[raw/articles/wikipedia-mother-earth-american-band.md] The **British** Mother Earth (1991–1996, Acid Jazz Records) came two decades later — an acid-jazz outfit whose organist Bryn Barklam did in fact play Hammond (he later played organ for Buzzcocks and fronts an organ trio pointedly named Captain Hammond) — but their records are 1990s acid jazz, not the 1968/1971 albums the query points at.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-mother-earth-british-band.md]

**The Hammond role in the US band:** keyboard chairs rotated — early members Wayne Talbert and Ira Kamin (1967 lineup), then **Mark Naftalin** (ex-Paul Butterfield Blues Band) on piano, organ and keyboards in the 1968 lineup that made *Living with the Animals*; session organ by Barry Goldberg also appears on the album.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-mother-earth-american-band.md]^[raw/articles/rockasteria-mother-earth-living-with-animals.md] On record the organ is a soul-blues support voice behind Nelson's vocals rather than a lead instrument in the Lord/Emerson sense. **Confidence: low-medium** — the band's Wikipedia article carries a citations-needed banner, and the exact organ chair on each track of *Living with the Animals* (Naftalin vs Goldberg) rests on the album's liner credits as relayed by a collector blog, not a scholarly source.

## Santana — Gregg Rolie: the Hammond in dialogue with the guitar

Rolie (b. 1947, Seattle) was a founding member and original lead vocalist of Santana, joining Carlos Santana in 1966 in the Santana Blues Band; he later co-founded Journey with Neal Schon in 1973, and is a two-time Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inductee (Santana 1998, Journey 2017).^[raw/articles/wikipedia-gregg-rolie.md] The 1969 debut *Santana* — boosted by Woodstock six days before release, spending 108 weeks on the Billboard chart — has Rolie's organ as the harmonic glue: fills and answer phrases on the Top 10 hit "Evil Ways" and his co-written "Soul Sacrifice".^[raw/articles/wikipedia-santana-1969-album.md]^[raw/articles/wikipedia-gregg-rolie.md] On *Abraxas* (1970) the Rolie organ-vs-Carlos-guitar dialogue is the album's core texture — under "Black Magic Woman" and "Oye Como Va" — and on "Incident at Neshabur" his keyboards are deliberately contrasted with guest pianist Alberto Gianquinto's jazz-influenced style.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-abraxas-album.md] *Santana III* (1971), the last studio album of the classic Woodstock lineup, is the densest round of that duelling.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-gregg-rolie.md]

## Synthesis — what the Hammond does differently in each idiom

Four acts, four jobs for the same tonewheel engine (all rooted in the R&B/jazz organ tradition of [[jimmy-smith|Jimmy Smith]], [[jimmy-mcgriff|Jimmy McGriff]] and McDuff that both Lord and Emerson cite):

- **Hard rock (Lord/Deep Purple):** the organ is made to *replace a rhythm guitar* — overdriven through Marshalls, playing riffs, comping rhythms and duelling with the lead guitar at equal volume. Distortion is the point; the [[drawbars|drawbars]] and percussion are pushed toward an aggressive, industrial reedy voice.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-jon-lord.md]
- **Prog (Emerson/ELP):** the organ is made to *replace an orchestra and a stunt section* — classical adaptations, suites, plus physical theatre on a cheap L-100, with the Moog taking over lead duties the Hammond could not carry. The [[tonewheel-generator|tonewheel]] sound here is one keyboard voice among several in a rigged arsenal.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-keith-emerson.md]^[raw/articles/wikipedia-emerson-lake-palmer.md]
- **Blues-rock (Mother Earth/Mark Naftalin):** the organ is a *supporting soul voice* — pads, fills and grooves behind a powerful vocalist, in the church-and-Chicago tradition rather than the conservatory one.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-mother-earth-american-band.md]
- **Latin rock (Rolie/Santana):** the organ is a *conversational partner* — answering the guitar in call-and-response inside percussion-driven grooves, adding the jazz-chord vocabulary that lets a dance band stretch out instrumentally.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-abraxas-album.md]

The common thread: in every case the instrument escaped the jazz trio and the church and became a *rock band member* — but each band re-weighted the same recipe (drawbars + Leslie + volume) toward a different function.
