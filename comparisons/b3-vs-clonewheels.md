# Hammond B-3 vs Clonewheels

A clonewheel organ emulates the electromechanical Hammond with solid-state or DSP/computer-chip circuitry instead of mechanical [[tonewheel-generator|tonewheels]] — trading the B-3's weight and fragility for portability and reliability.^[raw/articles/wikipedia-clonewheel-organ.md]

Web sources: [Korg CX-3 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korg_CX-3), [Nord C Series (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nord_C_Series)

## Verdict / synthesis

The generations matter. First-wave clones (Roland VK-1/9, early combo attempts) were criticized for unrealistic upper harmonics and rotary simulation, and jazz/blues players kept vintage Hammonds for recordings ^[raw/articles/wikipedia-clonewheel-organ.md]. The Korg CX-3 (1979) was the first lightweight organ with genuinely comparable sound — Gordon Reid called it close to "the true depth and passion of a vintage Hammond," especially through a real Leslie ^[raw/articles/wikipedia-clonewheel-organ.md]. By the 1990s–2000s, DSP modeling and sampling closed most of the audible gap: leakage, key click, tube growl and Leslie rotation are now explicitly simulated, and educated listeners can be fooled "especially if it's still going through a Leslie" (Goldings) ^[raw/articles/npr-blogsupreme-hammond-b3.md].

The practical trade is decisive: clonewheels dominate **touring and club work** (weight, reliability, cost), while vintage B-3s retain prestige and a following for **studio and gospel/jazz work**, where the wooden instrument's look, springy patented key feel, and unpredictable vintage character are part of the music. Goldings also notes that vintage Hammonds vary from unit to unit like pianos — a variability clones deliberately normalize. The remaining honest gap is haptic (the keyboard action) and the physical Leslie, not the tone generation. ^[raw/articles/npr-blogsupreme-hammond-b3.md] ^[raw/articles/wikipedia-clonewheel-organ.md]
